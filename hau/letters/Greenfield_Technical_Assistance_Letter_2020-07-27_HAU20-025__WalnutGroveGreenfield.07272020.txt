Through the State’s Housing Element Law, the legislature established that, “the
availability of housing is of vital statewide importance, and the early attainment of
decent housing and a suitable living environment for every Californian, including
farmworkers, is a priority of the highest order” (Gov. Code, § 65580, emphasis
added).

The City of Greenfield achieves this through its adopted specific housing programs
in its housing element. These programs commit to facilitate the development of
housing for agricultural workers and utilize CEQA 1 exemptions where possible to
facilitate this housing. Specifically, with emphasis added:

      •   Program 6.4L commits to “Identify potential funding sources and
          development sites and work with nonprofit developers to facilitate the
          development of affordable farmworker housing, senior housing, and housing
          for the physically and developmentally disabled.
      •   Program 6.4B commits to “Revise the Zoning Ordinance to allow farm labor
          and employee housing for up to six employees in residential zones within the
          City, and review, and if necessary, remove barriers to the development of
          other housing appropriate for agricultural workers and other employees.”
      •   Program 6.2.G commits to “Utilize California Environmental Quality Act
          exemptions for affordable housing and infill housing projects to the extent
          allowable.”
      •   Program 6.4.J commits to “Utilize California Environmental Quality Act
          exemptions for agricultural worker housing, and housing for the elderly and
          persons with physical and developmental disabilities, to the extent
          allowable.”

The Walnut Grove Apartment II & III housing project would aid the City in meeting its
obligations outlined in these housing element programs, as well as complete its
remaining lower-income RHNA goals for this cycle of 38 very low- and low-income units.

Requiring comprehensive CEQA review given the very limited scope of the City’s
residual discretion and given its commitments under the housing element – rather than
utilizing an exemption – potentially delays the project during a pandemic, delaying
sorely needed housing options for a vulnerable and essential population. Requiring
expensive CEQA studies, when the City cannot condition the project legally on the
results of those studies, undermines the City’s implementation of Program 6.4 and is
inconsistent with CEQA.

Housing Accountability Act

In enacting the Housing Accountability Act, the Legislature declared, “California has
a housing supply and affordability crisis of historic proportions” (Gov. Code, §
65589.5, subd. (a)(2)(A)). This housing crisis, the Legislature found, has adverse
impacts on Californians and California, impacting citizens’ health, safety, economic
standing and the state’s environment and competitiveness (Gov. Code, § 65589.5,
subd. (a)(2)). Through the Housing Accountability Act, the Legislature intended “to
significantly increase the approval and construction of new housing for all economic
segments of California’s communities by meaningfully and effectively curbing the
capability of local governments to deny, reduce the density for, or render infeasible
housing developments” (Gov. Code, § 65589.5, subd. (a)(2)(K)). Furthermore, the
Legislature declared, “It is the policy of the state that this section should be
interpreted and implemented in a manner to afford the fullest possible weight to the
interest of, and the approval and provision of, housing” (Gov. Code, § 65589.5,
subd. (a)(2)(L)). Because of this housing crisis, the Housing Accountability Act
creates substantive preconditions for the delay and disapproval of affordable
housing.

On November 1, 2019, the City sent a letter to the applicant, determining that the
application was complete. The Walnut Grove Apartments II & III housing
development is a permitted use on the proposed site, subject only to design review
under the City’s zoning code. Nevertheless, the City’s November 1 letter stated that
the project application would require environmental review in advance of Design
Review by the Planning Commission. The letter did not provide details as to the
form that review would take (i.e., exemption, negative declaration, mitigated
negative declaration, or environmental impact report). It was not until March 31,
2020 that the City informed the applicant that CEQA review would take the form of
a negative declaration. The City asserts that comprehensive review is required
because the City needs to evaluate whether the project is in “conformance with all
applicable provisions of this title.” (Letter to Art Coon, Miller Starr, from James
Sanchez, Lozano Smith, dated April 20, 2020, page 2).

Under the Housing Accountability Act, the City was under a mandatory statutory
duty to provide the applicant with notice that it “considers a proposed housing
development project to be inconsistent, non-compliant, or not in conformity with any
applicable plan, program , policy, ordinance, standard, requirement, or other similar
provisions…” within 30 days of the date the application was determined to be
complete. (Gov. Code, § 65589.5, subd. (j)(2)(A)). As the Department understands

Housing Discrimination Law

The Salinas Valley is one of the most highly productive agricultural areas in the
State of California and the nation. There is a significant shortage and a significant
demand for farmworker housing in the area and in the City of Greenfield. The
Farmworker Housing Study and Action Plan for Salinas Valley and Pajaro Valley
found that an additional 45,560 units of farmworker housing are needed to alleviate
critical overcrowding in farmworker households.

Please keep in mind that local agencies are prohibited not only from exercising bias
based on race, sex, familial status, age, or religion, but from discriminating against any
individual or group of individuals the enjoyment of residence, landownership, tenancy,
or any other land use because the residential development is subsidized, multi-
family, or occupancy will include low- or moderate-income persons. 2 Local
agencies may also not impose different requirements on assisted developments than
those imposed on non-assisted projects. (Gov. Code, § 65008, subd. (b)(1)(D), and
subd. (d)(1-2)). However, they may extend preferential treatment to local, state or
federally assisted developments, and developments intended for occupancy by low-
and moderate-income households or agricultural employees. This preferential
treatment may include, but is not limited to, reduction of fees, changes in architectural
requirements, site development and property line requirements, building setback
requirements, or vehicle parking requirements that reduce development costs of these
developments. (Gov. Code, § 65008, subd. (e)(2)).
