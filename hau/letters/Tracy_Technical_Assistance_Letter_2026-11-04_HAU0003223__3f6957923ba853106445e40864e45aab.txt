         RE:       City of Tracy – Builder’s Remedy Provision of the Housing Accountability
                   Act – Letter of Technical Assistance

         The California Department of Housing and Community Development (HCD) received a
         request for technical assistance from the City of Tracy (City) regarding the builder’s
         remedy provisions of the Housing Accountability Act (HAA). 1 Specifically, the City asks
         whether deed restricting Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs) for rent in an otherwise for-
         sale development satisfies the affordability requirements for builder’s remedy
         developments. This letter clarifies that such an approach does not allow a project to
         qualify for the benefits of the builder’s remedy.

         Background

         HCD understands that the applicant has proposed four housing development projects
         totaling 3,084 units of which 2,067 are single family units and 1,017 units are proposed
         as ADUs. The projects are single-family subdivisions where lots would generally contain
         a single-family home and an attached ADU, according to the applicant. In each of the
         four projects, all ADUs are proposed to be deed restricted for lower income households,
         and no primary single-family units are proposed to be deed restricted. The applicant has
         submitted SB 330 preliminary applications for each of the four projects while the City did
         not have a Housing Element found in compliance by HCD. The City is currently
         processing a full development application for each of the projects. As a result, the
         applicant asserts that each project qualifies as a “builder’s remedy project.” 2 The
         projects vested after AB 1893 (Chapter 268, Statutes of 2025) came into effect.

       In a letter to HCD, the applicant asserted that while the HAA requires that affordable
       units must be deed restricted, it does not require that these affordable units be rented to
       qualifying households. According to the applicant, single family homes with attached,
       deed-restricted, low-income ADUs could be sold to individual, private buyers, who could
       then lawfully leave the low-income ADU vacant or used for other purposes (e.g., guest
       house, pool house, personal storage, etc.).

       In a letter submitted to HCD seeking technical assistance, the City wrote that it “does
       not believe that a single-family tract subdivision proposal with ADUs, even if they are
       constructed on the single-family lots, meets the definition of a ‘builder’s remedy project’
       if the owners are not actually required to provide the affordable housing to eligible
       households at an affordable rent for the required 55-year period.” The City informed the
       applicant that future buyers of the lots must actually rent the ADUs to income eligible
       households for the project entitlement process to proceed. Specifically, the City
       envisions that the applicant will need to record a deed restriction – in addition to the
       household income restriction on the ADUs – that informs owners of purchased lots that
       contain deed-restricted, affordable units of their affirmative obligation to lease out those
       affordable units.

       Analysis

       Without appropriate assurances that the ADU units will be made available to,
       rented to, and occupied by low-income households throughout the 55-year
       period, the proposed projects would not qualify under the Builder’s Remedy

       The four projects at the subject of this letter do not meet the statutory requirements in
       the HAA to qualify as a “builder’s remedy project” as proposed. A “builder’s remedy
       project” is defined as “a housing development project that provides housing for very low,
       low-, or moderate-income households.” 3 “A housing development project that provides
       housing for very low, low-, or moderate-income households” is defined to encompass
       “housing for mixed-income households.”4 The definition for “housing for mixed-income
       households” requires that the development proponent “shall agree to, and the local
       agency shall ensure, the continued affordability of all affordable rental units included
       pursuant to [the HAA] for 55 years.” 5

       Regarding the obligation to facilitate housing affordability, the applicant argues that this
       provision “does not require that affirmative steps must be taken to ensure an affordable
       rental unit is actually rented at all times during this period” (April 3, 2026, letter to HCD).
       In other words, the applicant contends that a builder’s remedy project may deed restrict
       rental ADUs on lots with single-family units, sell those lots to individual homebuyers,
       and leave those ADUs unoccupied by income-qualifying households.

       This interpretation is incorrect. “A housing development project that provides housing for
       very low, low-, or moderate-income households” 6 (emphasis added) means that there is
       an affirmative obligation to rent or sell the unit to qualified households rather than
       merely imposing deed restrictions. If, for example, units are left vacant for storage, used
       as pool houses, or rented to households that are not income eligible, they are not
       providing housing for lower-income households. This understanding of the phrase
       “provides housing for [qualified households]” is necessary to implement the provision’s
       plain meaning, as well as to interpret the HAA “in a manner to afford the fullest possible
       weight to the interest of, and the approval and provision of, housing.”7 (Emphasis
       added.)

       The project does not meet comparability requirements for affordable units.

       Beyond the projects’ ineligibility as builder’s remedy projects explained above, the
       projects also appear to violate the requirement that “affordable units in the development
       project shall have a comparable bedroom and bathroom count as the market rate
       units.” 8 According to documentation provided by the City, the two projects located on
       Tracy Boulevard have either three- or four-bedroom primary units (i.e., the market-rate
       units), while all ADUs (i.e., the affordable units) only include one bedroom. The two
       other projects – Corral Hollow and Westside Ranch – also only propose one-bedroom
       ADUs, although the bedroom counts for primary units were not made available.

       The affordable component of a builder’s remedy project must be comparably designed
       to the rest of the project and provided to qualifying households.

       Using deed-restricted rental ADUs in for-sale developments in the SDBL and HAA
       can be problematic.

       HCD has previously provided technical assistance letters about deed-restricted ADUs in
       the context of the State Density Bonus Law (SDBL). 9 The SDBL uses language that
       mirrors the HAA in requiring that applicants “shall agree to, and the city, county, or city
       and county shall ensure, the continued affordability” of affordable units that qualified a
       development for a density bonus. 10 In a Technical Assistance Letter to the City of
       Wildomar, HCD discussed a proposed single-family subdivision where the primary units
       would be for sale while the deed-restricted ADUs would be rented out by the primary
       units’ buyers (like the four proposed projects that are the subject of this letter). 11 The
       Wildomar letter discusses how using deed-restricted ADUs to qualify for the SDBL is not
       an unconditional right, but a choice that comes with responsibilities. The ability of deed-

restricted ADUs to qualify for the SDBL is contingent on the applicant’s ability to ensure
that deed-restricted affordable units are built and continuously occupied by households
at qualifying income levels. There, HCD advised against the use of deed-restricted
ADUs to qualify for the SDBL in a project where primary units are for sale and deed-

Significant concerns arise when housing development proponents attempt to deed
restrict rental ADUs in for-sale developments to become eligible for certain California
state housing laws, many of which confer benefits to the development proponent in
exchange for affordability. Future homeowners may not be able to effectively ensure
compliance with deed restrictions or verify the incomes of prospective tenants. The
homeowners may also be incentivized to leave units vacant or use them for other
purposes instead of renting them to eligible tenants, due to the real or perceived
challenges of managing deed-restricted affordable housing. Such an approach may also
expose future individual homebuyers to enforceable liabilities they may not fully
understand at the time of purchase. For these practical reasons, HCD advises against
the use of deed-restricted, affordable rental ADUs in for-sale housing developments as
a method to qualify for the benefits of the HAA, SDBL, and potentially other state
housing laws.

Conclusion

As proposed, the four housing development projects that are the subject of this letter do
not qualify as builder’s remedy projects under the HAA for the reasons stated above.
HCD remains committed to supporting the City of Tracy in facilitating housing at all
income levels and hopes the City finds this clarification helpful. If you have questions or
need additional information, please contact Brandon Yung at
brandon.yung@hcd.ca.gov.
